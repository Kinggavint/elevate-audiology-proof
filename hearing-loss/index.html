Common causes

What causes hearing loss?

A number of factors can damage hearing — some are everyday and preventable, others are genetic or medical.

  • Aging (presbycusis) — the most common cause
  • Noise exposure from work, recreation, or sudden loud events
  • Ear infections (acute or chronic)
  • Excessive earwax blocking the canal
  • Trauma to the ear or head
  • Genetics & birth defects
  • Medical conditions like otosclerosis or Ménière's
  • Ototoxic medications that damage the inner ear
Three categories

The three types of hearing loss

Type 1

Sensorineural

Damage to the inner ear (cochlea) or auditory nerve. Permanent and the most common form — typically managed with hearing aids.

Type 2

Conductive

Sound can't reach the inner ear because of a problem in the outer or middle ear. Often medically or surgically treatable.

Type 3

Mixed

A combination of sensorineural and conductive loss. Treatment depends on which component is dominant.

A specific case

Single-sided deafness (SSD)

Single-sided deafness — also called unilateral hearing loss — is when one ear has hearing loss while the other hears normally. It's diagnosed in about 60,000 Americans each year.

While there's no cure and traditional amplification is less effective, alternative treatment options like CROS hearing aids and bone-anchored devices can dramatically improve everyday hearing.

  • Trouble understanding speech in noisy environments
  • Fatigue from straining to listen
  • Avoiding social situations

Answers from our audiologists

Hearing loss FAQs

Learn about early signs, common causes, treatment options, and what to expect from a comprehensive hearing evaluation.

What are the early signs of hearing loss?

Hearing loss rarely announces itself all at once. Most people notice small changes first: asking others to repeat themselves more often, turning the TV up louder than you used to, struggling to follow conversations in noisy places like restaurants, or feeling like people are mumbling even when they are not. You might find yourself avoiding certain social situations because they feel exhausting or frustrating. Tinnitus, a ringing or buzzing sound in the ears, can also be an early indicator.

If any of these sound familiar, trust that instinct. The earlier hearing loss is identified, the more options you have. A comprehensive hearing evaluation at Elevate Audiology takes about an hour and gives you a clear, complete picture of where your hearing stands today.

What causes hearing loss?

Hearing loss has many possible causes, and in some cases more than one factor is at play. The most common include:

  • Age-related changes to the inner ear, which occur gradually over time
  • Noise exposure, whether from a single loud event or years of cumulative exposure to loud environments
  • Genetics, since hearing loss can run in families
  • Certain medications that are known to be toxic to the hearing system
  • Ear infections or fluid behind the eardrum
  • Head or ear trauma
  • Medical conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or autoimmune disorders

Understanding the cause matters because it shapes your treatment options. At Elevate Audiology, we do not just test your hearing; we take a thorough case history to understand the full picture before making any recommendations.

What is the difference between sensorineural and conductive hearing loss?

These are the two main types of hearing loss, and they involve different parts of the hearing system.

Sensorineural hearing loss occurs when there is damage to the tiny hair cells inside the cochlea (the inner ear) or to the auditory nerve that carries sound signals to the brain. This is the most common type in adults and is typically permanent. Noise exposure and aging are the most frequent causes. Hearing aids are the primary treatment and are highly effective.

Conductive hearing loss occurs when something is blocking or interfering with the transmission of sound through the outer or middle ear. This could be earwax buildup, fluid behind the eardrum, a perforated eardrum, or a problem with the small bones in the middle ear. Conductive hearing loss is often temporary and can sometimes be treated medically or surgically.

Some people have a combination of both types, which is called mixed hearing loss.

Knowing which type you have is one of the most important things a hearing evaluation can tell you. Our team at Elevate Audiology uses comprehensive diagnostic testing to identify exactly what is happening and why, so you get the right recommendation, not a one-size-fits-all answer.

How does aging affect hearing?

Age-related hearing loss, clinically called presbycusis, is one of the most common conditions affecting adults over 50, and it progresses gradually, which is part of why so many people do not notice it until it has advanced. As we age, the hair cells in the inner ear naturally begin to deteriorate. These cells do not regenerate, which is why age-related hearing loss is permanent.

It typically affects high-frequency sounds first. This is why speech may still seem loud enough but feel unclear, particularly in noisy environments or when someone is not facing you directly.

The good news is that today's hearing technology is more sophisticated than most people realize. Modern hearing aids are designed specifically to address the patterns of age-related hearing loss, helping restore clarity, not just volume. If you are over 50 and have not had a hearing evaluation recently, it is worth knowing where you stand.

How does loud noise exposure cause hearing loss?

Sound is measured in decibels (dB). Sounds above 85 dB can begin to damage the hair cells in the inner ear with prolonged exposure, and sounds above 120 dB can cause damage almost immediately. The damage is cumulative, meaning years of moderate noise exposure adds up the same way years of sun exposure does.

Common sources of damaging noise include:

  • Concerts and live music events
  • Power tools and lawn equipment
  • Firearms and hunting
  • Earbuds or headphones at high volume
  • Occupational noise in manufacturing, construction, or agriculture

What makes noise-induced hearing loss particularly important to understand is that it is entirely preventable, but it is not reversible. Once those hair cells are gone, they do not come back. Hearing protection, used consistently and correctly, is the most effective defense.

If you have a history of significant noise exposure, a baseline hearing evaluation is one of the smartest things you can do for your long-term health. Our team at Elevate Audiology can assess any existing damage and talk with you about protecting the hearing you have.

When do I actually need a hearing aid?

This is one of the most common questions we hear, and the honest answer is: sooner than most people think. Research consistently shows that people wait an average of seven years from the time they first notice changes to when they seek help. That is seven years of miscommunication, social withdrawal, and cognitive strain that did not have to happen.

You may benefit from hearing aids if:

  • You regularly ask people to repeat themselves
  • You struggle to follow conversations in background noise
  • Family members or friends have commented on your hearing
  • You have been told you turn the TV up too loud
  • You feel mentally tired after social events or long conversations
  • A hearing evaluation has shown measurable hearing loss

Hearing aids are not just about volume. Today's technology is designed to help the brain process speech more clearly, reduce listening fatigue, and improve your ability to function in the real-world environments that matter most to you. At Elevate Audiology, we use a functional hearing assessment to identify your specific goals before recommending any device, so you know exactly what you are getting and why.

Is hearing loss treatable, or is it permanent?

It depends on the type and the cause. Conductive hearing loss caused by earwax, fluid, infection, or certain structural issues is often treatable medically and may be fully reversible. Sensorineural hearing loss, the most common type in adults, is typically permanent because it involves hair cells that do not regenerate.

However, permanent does not mean unmanageable. Hearing aids, when properly fitted and programmed to your specific hearing profile, can significantly restore your ability to communicate, engage socially, and reduce the mental fatigue that untreated hearing loss causes. For some patients with severe to profound loss, cochlear implants may be an option worth exploring with your audiologist and physician.

What we know with certainty is that untreated hearing loss has real consequences: it is associated with cognitive decline, depression, social isolation, and falls in older adults. Getting an evaluation is not a commitment to treatment. It is information, and information puts you in control.

How do I know if I need a medical referral or a hearing aid?

This is exactly what a comprehensive audiological evaluation is designed to answer. Our role at Elevate Audiology is not simply to sell hearing aids. It is to understand what is happening with your hearing, determine the most likely cause, and point you toward the right next step, whether that is hearing technology, a referral to an ENT or physician, monitoring over time, or a combination.

If your evaluation reveals something that warrants medical attention, we will tell you clearly and refer you appropriately. If hearing aids are the right fit, we will show you why with real measurements, not a sales pitch.

Can children have hearing loss, or is it just an adult issue?

Hearing loss affects people of all ages. In children, it can significantly impact speech and language development, academic performance, and social development. Warning signs in children include delayed speech milestones, difficulty following directions, frequently asking for repetition, or seeming inattentive in school.

At Elevate Audiology, we evaluate patients starting from childhood. Early identification is critical because the brain's ability to adapt and develop around hearing loss is greatest in the early years.

What should I expect at my first hearing evaluation?

A first appointment at Elevate Audiology typically includes:

  • A thorough review of your health and hearing history
  • A discussion of your goals: what is hard, what matters most to you, and what you want to be able to do better
  • A complete audiogram measuring your hearing across all frequencies
  • Speech testing to measure how well you understand words, not just whether you can hear them
  • Additional testing as appropriate based on your history and results
  • A clear explanation of your results in plain language
  • An honest conversation about your options, with no pressure

We believe you deserve to understand exactly what is happening with your hearing before you make any decisions. That is what we are here for.
